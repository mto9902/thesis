# Proposal Draft

## Title

**Recognition, Stackability, and Student Trust in IT Micro-Credentials in Thailand: A Survey-Experimental Study of Perceived Value among Thai IT Students**

## Plain-language study description

This study examines what makes Thai IT students believe that an IT micro-credential is legitimate, useful, and worth pursuing.

## Introduction

Micro-credentials have become an important part of current debates on lifelong learning, employability, and flexible higher education. Unlike traditional degrees, micro-credentials usually certify smaller units of learning, often delivered through online or blended formats. Their appeal lies in their flexibility, shorter duration, and closer alignment with rapidly changing skill demands. International policy bodies increasingly view micro-credentials as a way to support upskilling and reskilling in fast-moving sectors, especially where traditional degree structures may respond more slowly to labor-market change. OECD (2021) notes that the number and diversity of micro-credential offerings have expanded substantially in recent years, while UNESCO Bangkok highlighted in September 2025 that micro-credentials are becoming increasingly important to flexible lifelong learning systems in Asia-Pacific.

The issue is especially relevant to IT and digital fields. Students in computer science, software engineering, information systems, and related majors often face rapidly evolving skill requirements. Employers frequently value current technical capabilities, portfolios, tools, and specialized competencies that may not be fully captured by a conventional curriculum alone. In this context, IT micro-credentials appear promising because they can signal narrower and more current skills in areas such as data analytics, cybersecurity, cloud computing, software tools, and AI-related applications. However, the practical value of such credentials is not automatically obvious to students. Many students may ask whether a micro-credential is genuinely credible, whether it will be recognized by formal institutions, and whether it can contribute to larger educational or career pathways.

Thailand provides a timely and important setting for this research. The policy environment already reflects strong interest in more flexible skills pathways. OECD Skills Strategy Thailand (2025) reports that Thai MOOC has grown to more than 1.4 million users, with more than 1.3 million course completions and certificate awards, while the National Credit Bank System has been developed to encourage wider participation in lifelong learning and make it easier for learners to convert prior learning into credit. At the same time, both OECD and UNESCO sources emphasize that trust, recognition, and portability are central challenges in micro-credential ecosystems. The existence of micro-credentials alone does not guarantee that learners will see them as valuable.

This thesis therefore focuses on the student side of the problem, but in a stronger way than a typical adoption study. Instead of asking only whether students intend to use micro-credentials, the study examines which specific design signals make a micro-credential appear credible and worthwhile. The proposed design tests the causal effects of three visible signals: formal recognition, stackability, and industry endorsement. These signals are expected to shape student trust, which in turn shapes perceived value. The study is positioned at the intersection of educational technology, higher education reform, skills policy, and IT-related workforce development.

## Statement of the Problem

Although micro-credentials are increasingly promoted as tools for lifelong learning and employability, their value is not self-evident to students. In Thailand, current policy developments such as Thai MOOC and the National Credit Bank System suggest institutional momentum toward shorter and more flexible forms of learning. However, there is still limited evidence on what makes IT micro-credentials appear credible and valuable from the perspective of Thai IT students.

A major problem is that students cannot directly verify the true labor-market and educational value of a micro-credential before investing their time and effort. Instead, they must rely on visible cues. These may include whether the credential is formally recognized, whether it can be stacked toward larger qualifications, and whether it is endorsed by industry actors. Without stronger evidence on which of these signals matter most, universities, platform providers, and policymakers may continue expanding micro-credential offerings without understanding how students judge their legitimacy and usefulness.

Existing student-side studies often frame the issue as generic technology acceptance or adoption. While that approach has value, it may be too narrow for a policy-relevant question about credential quality, recognition, and educational trust. This study addresses that gap by testing how concrete credential features shape student trust and perceived value in the context of IT micro-credentials in Thailand.

## Research Objectives

- To examine the effect of formal recognition on student trust in IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students.
- To examine the effect of stackability on student trust in IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students.
- To examine the effect of industry endorsement on student trust in IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students.
- To examine the effect of student trust on the perceived value of IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students.
- To examine the direct effects of formal recognition, stackability, and industry endorsement on the perceived value of IT micro-credentials.
- To examine whether student trust mediates the relationship between these credential signals and perceived value.

## Research Questions

- RQ1: How does formal recognition affect student trust in IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students?
- RQ2: How does stackability affect student trust in IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students?
- RQ3: How does industry endorsement affect student trust in IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students?
- RQ4: How does student trust affect the perceived value of IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students?
- RQ5: Do formal recognition, stackability, and industry endorsement directly influence perceived value?
- RQ6: Does student trust mediate the relationship between these credential signals and perceived value?

## Theoretical Framework

The study is primarily grounded in Signaling Theory. In simple terms, signaling theory explains how people make judgments when they cannot directly observe the true quality of something. Applied to this study, students cannot directly know whether a micro-credential will actually help them academically or professionally, so they infer quality and value from visible signals such as formal recognition, stackability, and industry endorsement.

The study is also informed by institutional trust and legitimacy perspectives. These help explain why recognition matters. When a credential is recognized by universities, credit systems, or respected organizations, students are more likely to see it as legitimate and reliable. Human capital theory serves as a secondary lens by explaining why students care about credentials that may improve skills, employability, and future opportunities.

Taken together, these perspectives support a model in which recognition, stackability, and industry endorsement act as credibility signals; these signals influence student trust; and student trust helps shape perceived value.

## Hypotheses

- H1: Formal recognition positively affects student trust in IT micro-credentials.
- H2: Stackability positively affects student trust in IT micro-credentials.
- H3: Industry endorsement positively affects student trust in IT micro-credentials.
- H4: Student trust positively affects perceived value.
- H5: Formal recognition positively affects perceived value.
- H6: Stackability positively affects perceived value.
- H7: Industry endorsement positively affects perceived value.
- H8: Student trust mediates the effect of formal recognition on perceived value.
- H9: Student trust mediates the effect of stackability on perceived value.
- H10: Student trust mediates the effect of industry endorsement on perceived value.

## Methodology

This study will use a quantitative, vignette-based survey experiment with a 2 x 2 x 2 design. Respondents will be randomly assigned to one of eight hypothetical IT micro-credential scenarios. The experimental manipulations will vary three features: formal recognition, stackability, and industry endorsement. This design allows the study to test the causal effect of concrete credential characteristics rather than relying only on abstract opinions.

The target population is Thai undergraduate and postgraduate students in IT, computer science, software engineering, information systems, and related digital majors. The study will use purposive and convenience sampling through multiple universities, student communities, course networks, and online distribution channels. The target is 480 usable responses, with a minimum acceptable sample of 320 and a target of 60 respondents per experimental cell.

The questionnaire will contain five sections: screening and demographics, one randomized vignette, manipulation checks, trust items, perceived value items, and optional exploratory items for enrollment intention. Data analysis will include randomization checks, reliability testing, descriptive statistics, OLS regression or factorial ANOVA for experimental effects, and bootstrap mediation analysis to test the mediating role of trust. Exploratory subgroup analysis may compare undergraduate versus postgraduate students and students with versus without prior MOOC experience.

## Expected Contribution

### Theoretical contribution

This study shifts the focus away from generic adoption models and toward the signaling and legitimacy logic of micro-credentials. It contributes to the emerging literature by explaining how learners infer value from visible credential features rather than assuming that value is already clear. It also extends current discussion by treating trust as a central mechanism between credential design and perceived value.

### Practical contribution

The findings can guide universities, online learning providers, and Thai policymakers in designing micro-credentials that students regard as credible and worthwhile. If certain features consistently improve trust and perceived value, those features can become practical priorities in future credential design, recognition systems, and short-course strategies.

### Scholarship-facing contribution

The project is positioned as a Thailand-based study of a globally relevant issue: how new forms of short-form certification can become trusted, legible, and meaningful within evolving skills ecosystems. This gives the thesis relevance beyond a single institution or platform and helps frame it as a contribution to broader debates in educational technology, higher education reform, and digital workforce development.
