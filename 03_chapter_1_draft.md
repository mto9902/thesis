# Chapter 1 Draft

## Chapter 1: Introduction

### 1.1 Background of Study

Micro-credentials have become a major topic in higher education, skills policy, and digital learning. They are commonly understood as short-form learning certifications that verify specific learning outcomes, competencies, or skills. Compared with traditional degree programs, micro-credentials are usually narrower, more flexible, and faster to complete. Their rise is closely tied to changing labor-market needs, rapid technological change, and growing demand for lifelong learning. According to OECD (2021), the number and diversity of micro-credential offerings expanded substantially in recent years, especially after the COVID-19 period accelerated online and flexible learning models.

At the international level, micro-credentials are increasingly discussed not only as learning tools but also as policy instruments. The European Commission states that micro-credentials can support lifelong learning and employability, but it also stresses that they need quality, transparency, recognition, and portability if they are to reach their full potential. In Asia-Pacific, UNESCO Bangkok emphasized in its September 24, 2025 event on micro-credentials that the success of these credentials depends on clear recognition by governments, institutions, and employers. This means that the value of a micro-credential is not created by short-form learning alone. Its broader usefulness depends on whether learners believe it is credible, trusted, and meaningful within education and employment systems.

These issues are particularly important in IT-related education. Information technology, computer science, software engineering, and digital business fields change quickly. Students in these disciplines often need more agile ways to build current and specialized skills beyond the boundaries of formal degree curricula. IT micro-credentials may offer one response to this challenge because they can represent short, targeted learning in areas such as cybersecurity, cloud systems, data analytics, programming tools, AI applications, and digital project work. However, the presence of an IT micro-credential does not automatically mean that students will see it as worthwhile. Students may question whether such credentials are formally recognized, whether they can contribute to future study pathways, and whether they carry credible signals of labor-market value.

Thailand provides a highly relevant context for studying this issue. OECD Skills Strategy Thailand (2025) reports that Thailand has already developed policy instruments and digital learning platforms that reflect a national commitment to flexible learning and skills development. Thai MOOC, launched in 2016, is reported to have more than 1.4 million users and more than 1.3 million certificate-awarding completions. OECD also reports that the Ministry of Higher Education, Science, Research and Innovation has developed the National Credit Bank System to encourage participation in adult learning and support the conversion of previous learning and work experience into credits. These developments suggest that Thailand is not merely observing the global micro-credential trend from afar. It is actively building elements of a national ecosystem for shorter and more flexible forms of learning.

Despite this momentum, an important question remains unresolved. Even if policy actors create more short-course and credentialing pathways, how do learners decide whether a micro-credential is actually valuable? Students often cannot directly observe the future academic or labor-market payoff of a credential before they invest time, effort, and possibly money. Instead, they may rely on visible signals. Examples include whether the credential is officially recognized, whether it can be stacked into larger qualifications, and whether respected industry actors endorse it. These features may strongly shape student trust, and trust may in turn affect whether the credential is seen as valuable.

For this reason, the present study shifts the focus away from a narrow technology-adoption perspective and toward a credibility and value perspective. Rather than asking only whether students are willing to use IT micro-credentials, the study investigates what makes students trust them and see them as worthwhile. Specifically, it examines the effects of formal recognition, stackability, and industry endorsement on student trust and perceived value among Thai IT students.

### 1.2 Statement of Problem

Micro-credentials are increasingly promoted as a response to the need for flexible, targeted, and employment-relevant learning. In Thailand, digital learning platforms and policy initiatives indicate growing institutional support for shorter and more modular educational pathways. However, the expansion of offerings alone does not ensure that learners perceive those offerings as credible or valuable.

The central problem is that Thai IT students face uncertainty when judging the value of IT micro-credentials. Before taking a course or investing in a certificate, students often do not know whether the credential will be recognized by institutions, whether it can contribute to future qualifications, or whether it will carry meaningful labor-market credibility. Because the real payoff of the credential is uncertain at the time of decision-making, students may depend on visible quality signals when evaluating it.

Although previous research has examined student attitudes and adoption behavior in online learning contexts, less attention has been given to the causal role of concrete micro-credential features in shaping trust and perceived value. As a result, there is still insufficient evidence on which design features matter most to student judgment in the Thai context. Without this knowledge, universities, platform providers, and policymakers may continue developing micro-credentials without knowing how to make them more credible and more meaningful to learners.

### 1.3 Research Objectives

- To examine the effect of formal recognition on student trust in IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students.
- To examine the effect of stackability on student trust in IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students.
- To examine the effect of industry endorsement on student trust in IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students.
- To examine the effect of student trust on the perceived value of IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students.
- To examine the direct effects of formal recognition, stackability, and industry endorsement on the perceived value of IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students.
- To examine whether student trust mediates the relationship between these credential signals and perceived value.

### 1.4 Research Questions

- RQ1: How does formal recognition affect student trust in IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students?
- RQ2: How does stackability affect student trust in IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students?
- RQ3: How does industry endorsement affect student trust in IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students?
- RQ4: How does student trust affect the perceived value of IT micro-credentials among Thai IT students?
- RQ5: Do formal recognition, stackability, and industry endorsement directly influence perceived value?
- RQ6: Does student trust mediate the relationship between these credential signals and perceived value?

### 1.5 Scope of Research

This study focuses on IT micro-credentials in Thailand. The target population consists of undergraduate and postgraduate students enrolled in IT-related programs, including computer science, software engineering, information technology, information systems, and related digital majors at Thai universities.

The study examines three independent variables represented as experimental signals: formal recognition, stackability, and industry endorsement. Student trust serves as the mediating variable, while perceived value of IT micro-credentials is the primary dependent variable. Enrollment intention is treated as a secondary exploratory outcome.

Methodologically, the study adopts a quantitative survey-experimental design using randomized vignettes. Respondents will evaluate one hypothetical IT micro-credential scenario, and the analysis will focus on how different credential features influence trust and perceived value.

### 1.6 Research Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations. First, the respondent pool is limited to students rather than employers, which means the study captures perceived value from the learner side rather than actual hiring-side behavior. Second, the use of survey-based responses means that the study measures evaluated scenarios and stated perceptions rather than real enrollment or labor-market outcomes. Third, the sampling strategy is likely to rely on purposive and convenience recruitment across Thai universities, which may limit full population representativeness. Fourth, because the study is Thailand-focused, the findings may not be directly generalizable to other national contexts with different recognition systems and labor-market structures.

Despite these limitations, the study remains valuable because it addresses an important and underexplored question in a context where student judgment plays a major role in whether micro-credentials gain traction.

### 1.7 Significance of the Study

#### Theoretical significance

This study contributes to the emerging literature on micro-credentials by moving beyond a simple adoption framework and instead explaining how learners infer value from visible credential signals. It brings together signaling theory, institutional trust or legitimacy, and human capital logic to explain why some micro-credentials appear more credible than others. In doing so, it offers a stronger conceptual approach to learner-side evaluation of short-form credentials.

#### Practical significance

The findings can help universities, digital learning providers, and policymakers in Thailand design micro-credentials that students regard as legitimate and worthwhile. If recognition, stackability, or industry endorsement are shown to increase trust and perceived value, these features can be prioritized in future credential design and policy development. This may improve the effectiveness of short-course strategies, encourage participation, and strengthen the overall credibility of emerging micro-credential ecosystems.

#### Policy significance

At a broader level, the study is relevant to current discussions on lifelong learning, digital workforce development, and flexible higher education pathways in Thailand. As the country continues to expand digital learning platforms and credit-bank mechanisms, evidence on how students judge new credential forms can support better policy design and clearer communication around recognition and value.

### 1.8 Definition of Key Terms

**Micro-credentials**  
Short-form certifications that verify specific learning outcomes, competencies, or skills achieved through a focused learning experience.

**IT micro-credentials**  
Micro-credentials related to information technology and digital fields, such as programming, analytics, cybersecurity, cloud systems, software tools, or digital project skills.

**Formal recognition**  
The extent to which a micro-credential is officially acknowledged by universities, academic systems, or national learning-credit mechanisms.

**Stackability**  
The extent to which a micro-credential can be accumulated, combined, or transferred toward a larger qualification, certificate, or academic credit pathway.

**Industry endorsement**  
The extent to which a micro-credential is supported, reviewed, or validated by recognized industry bodies, professional associations, or employer-related groups.

**Student trust**  
Students' belief that an IT micro-credential is credible, reliable, legitimate, and worthy of confidence.

**Perceived value**  
Students' overall judgment that an IT micro-credential offers meaningful academic, professional, or personal benefit relative to the effort required to obtain it.

**Enrollment intention**  
Students' stated willingness to enroll in or pursue an IT micro-credential in the future.
